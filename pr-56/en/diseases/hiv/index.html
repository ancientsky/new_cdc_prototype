HIV infection Category 3 notifiable disease

HIV spreads through condomless sex, shared needles and from mother to child. Regular testing, PrEP and condoms are effective prevention; start treatment right after diagnosis, and an undetectable viral load means HIV cannot be transmitted (U=U).

What to do

If youhad unprotected sex or a needle-stick injury in the past 72 hours

See a doctor about PEP as soon as possible

Within 72 hours (the sooner the better), visit a designated PEP hospital; PEP is taken for 28 days.

If youmay be at risk and want to know your status

Get an anonymous test or self-test

Use anonymous testing hospitals, community testing sites or an HIV self-test kit; a positive result must be confirmed at a hospital.

If youare diagnosed with HIV

Start treatment promptly

Start antiretroviral treatment soon after diagnosis; keeping the virus undetectable means you will not pass it on through sex (U=U).

Symptoms and warning signs

Two to six weeks after infection, some people have flu-like acute symptoms: fever, sore throat, fatigue, rash and swollen lymph nodes, which usually pass within weeks.

There may then be no symptoms for years while the virus damages the immune system. Without treatment, opportunistic infections eventually occur (AIDS). Testing is the only way to know.

Warning signs — seek care immediately if any appear: Prolonged unexplained fever, rapid weight loss, persistent diarrhoea, white patches in the mouth or repeated pneumonia: see a doctor and ask for an HIV test.

How it spreads

HIV spreads in three ways:

  1. Sex: unprotected anal, vaginal or oral sex.
  2. Blood: sharing needles or diluents, or contaminated blood transfusion.
  3. Mother to child: during pregnancy, birth or breastfeeding.

Everyday contact such as handshakes, hugs, sharing meals or toilets, or mosquito bites does not spread HIV.

Prevention

  • Use condoms correctly every time, with water-based lubricant.
  • People at risk can ask about pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), which greatly reduces infection risk when taken as directed; some groups can receive government-funded PrEP.
  • Test regularly and know your and your partners' status.
  • Do not share needles or diluents; use needle exchange programmes.
  • All pregnant women are offered HIV testing; treatment during pregnancy reduces mother-to-child transmission to below 1%.

Diagnosis and treatment

HIV cannot yet be cured, but antiretroviral therapy (ART) controls the virus and people who take it regularly can expect a near-normal lifespan. Taiwan encourages starting treatment immediately after diagnosis, with drugs paid for by the government.

With an undetectable viral load (U=U, Undetectable = Untransmittable), HIV is not transmitted through sex.

Vaccine

There is no vaccine against HIV. Prevention relies on condoms, PrEP and regular testing. People living with HIV may be advised to receive hepatitis B, pneumococcal and mpox vaccines.

New HIV notifications in Taiwan have fallen year on year since 2017; see the dataset for monthly figures.
