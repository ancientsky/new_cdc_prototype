Influenza Category 4 notifiable disease

Influenza is caused by influenza viruses spread by droplets and contact, peaking in autumn and winter. Older adults, young children, pregnant women and people with chronic conditions are at higher risk of severe disease; an annual vaccine is the most effective protection.

What to do

If youhave sudden fever, body aches and cough

Wear a mask, see a doctor and rest at home

See a doctor early and stay home until 24 hours after symptoms improve; the doctor will assess whether antivirals are needed.

If youhave difficulty breathing, chest pain or confusion

Go to the emergency department now

These are danger signs of severe influenza. Seek care immediately.

If youare 65+, a young child, pregnant or have a chronic condition

Get the government-funded vaccine from 1 October

Government-funded flu vaccination starts on 1 October each year; find a contracted clinic near you.

Symptoms and warning signs

Common symptoms are sudden fever, headache, muscle aches, fatigue, runny nose, sore throat and cough; some children have diarrhoea or vomiting. Most people recover within about a week.

Unlike a common cold, influenza starts abruptly with marked whole-body symptoms and can lead to pneumonia, encephalitis or myocarditis.

Warning signs — seek care immediately if any appear: Rapid or difficult breathing, cyanosis (blue lips), bloody or thick sputum, chest pain, altered consciousness, low blood pressure; in children also high fever for more than 3 days, seizures or marked loss of activity.

How it spreads

Influenza spreads mainly through droplets from coughs and sneezes. It can also spread when hands touch secretions or contaminated objects and then the eyes, nose or mouth.

People may be infectious from 1 day before to 3–7 days after symptoms start; young children and immunocompromised people may shed virus longer.

Prevention

  • Get vaccinated every year: the virus changes and the vaccine is updated annually.
  • Wash hands often; cover coughs and sneezes with a sleeve or tissue.
  • Wear a mask when you have respiratory symptoms, stay home when sick and avoid crowds.
  • During flu season, make sure you have no fever or cough before visiting care homes or hospitals.

Diagnosis and treatment

Doctors may prescribe antivirals (oral oseltamivir, inhaled zanamivir, intravenous peramivir or oral baloxavir). They work best within 48 hours of onset, but severe or high-risk patients can still benefit later.

Taiwan CDC supplies government-funded antivirals for eligible patients; see the professional document on eligibility. Use paracetamol for fever and avoid aspirin in anyone under 18.

Vaccine

Government-funded flu vaccination starts in phases from 1 October each year for people aged 65 and over, children from 6 months to school entry, pregnant women, high-risk chronic patients, health and public health workers, and primary to senior high school students; adults aged 50–64 are included in the second phase.

Children aged 8 and under receiving flu vaccine for the first time need 2 doses at least 4 weeks apart. See the Influenza vaccine page for details.
